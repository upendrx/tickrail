What this engine does, step by step

Every number in the orange boxes below is taken live from the running engine, so you can follow each calculation with real data. If they change too fast to read, press Pause live numbers at the top.

The one-paragraph version

A feed adapter connects to a market-data source, here — for —. Adapters exist for crypto exchanges, US equities and CSV files, and all of them produce the same normalised messages. Every time the price or order book changes, the venue sends a small message. Our Rust engine turns each message into an up-to-date order book, works out what the price "really" is right now, and keeps one buy order just below that price and one sell order just above it. This is called market making. If both orders fill, we earn the gap between them. If the price moves through one of them, we lose. Before any order goes out, risk checks make sure we can't take a position that's too big. Orders are not sent to the venue. A simulator decides whether they would have filled, and we track the profit and loss as if they had.

First calculation: turning text into integers

Venues send prices as decimal text or JSON numbers, e.g. "83458.01000000". The adapter converts each one into a whole number of ticks (the smallest price step; for — one tick is —) and each size into a whole number of lots.

Why? Floating point can't store decimals like 0.1 exactly, so 0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3. On an exchange that means wrong prices, rejected orders and PnL that drifts. Whole numbers are exact and faster to compare. The parser reads the digits directly instead of going through a float, and rejects any price that isn't on the tick grid.

Tick and lot sizes are fetched from the venue's public REST API at start-up (for the CSV adapter they're set in the config), so the same code handles BTC at 0.01 ticks, a penny stock or a futures contract.

Step 2

Building the order book

The order book is the list of everyone waiting to trade. Bids are buyers, each saying "I'll buy up to this size at this price". Asks are sellers. The highest bid and the lowest ask are the top of book, and the gap between them is the spread.

How the engine keeps it up to date
  • A depth snapshot replaces the whole book (clear, then insert 40 levels).
  • Between snapshots, each bookTicker updates the best level and removes any level it proves stale. If the best bid falls from 100 to 99, level 100 must be gone.
  • Each side is a small array sorted so the best price is last. Most changes happen near the top, so an update touches only the end of the array: about 10 nanoseconds.
  • The strategy only looks at the book when a message batch is complete, so it never sees a half-updated book.

Step 3

The calculations, and why each one exists

The strategy is a market maker based on a well-known academic model (Avellaneda & Stoikov, 2008). It has one job: decide where to place its buy order and its sell order. Each calculation below feeds the next. The numbers are the engine's own values from its most recent decision. Prices inside formulas are in ticks; divide by 100 to get dollars.

3a · Mid price
—
Why? The simplest estimate of "the price": halfway between the best buyer and the best seller. It's a starting point, but it ignores how much size sits on each side.
3b · Microprice (our fair value)
—
Why? If there's a lot waiting to buy and very little waiting to sell, the ask is likely to be taken out and the price will tick up. The microprice weights each side by the other side's size, so it leans toward the thin side, where the price is about to move. It's a better short-term predictor than the mid, and it's what the strategy treats as the true price.
3c · Volatility (how jumpy the market is)
—
Why? When the price is moving fast, a resting order is more likely to be run over before we can move it. We measure recent volatility as an exponentially weighted average of squared mid changes: recent moves count most, and old ones fade out. The quotes then widen automatically when the market gets fast.
3d · Inventory skew (reservation price)
—
Why? A market maker wants to earn the spread, not bet on direction. If we've bought a lot (long), we move both quotes down: our sell becomes more attractive and our buy less so, which pushes us back toward flat. The price we centre on after this shift is the reservation price.
3e · Our target quotes
—
Why? We quote half the spread below and above the reservation price. The half-spread is a minimum distance plus a volatility term. Quotes are always kept on the passive side (never crossing the spread) and sent as post-only, so we're always the maker and never pay the spread.

When the strategy changes its orders

Cancelling and re-placing costs us: we lose our place in the queue at that price, and exchanges limit order rates. So the strategy only replaces an order when its target has moved by at least 2 ticks, and never more often than once every 10 ms per side. So far it has sent — orders and — cancels.

Step 5

Fills: how we decide an order "would have" traded

In paper mode no orders leave the process. A paper venue inside the engine decides fills from the real trades the feed reports. The rule is deliberately pessimistic:

  • Our resting buy at price P fills only when a real trade prints below P. A trade exactly at P doesn't count, because we can't know if we were first in the queue.
  • It also fills if the best ask drops to P or lower, meaning the market moved through our price.
  • The fill size is at most the size of the real trade that went through us.
This makes paper results more pessimistic than reality in one way (we get no fills at our exact price) and more optimistic in another (we assume zero delay between deciding and our order being in the book, and we ignore the market impact our orders would have had). Treat paper PnL as a lower-fidelity estimate; only real fills settle the question.

Step 6

Profit and loss: what the number means

Every fill changes two things: our position (how much BTC we hold, positive = long) and our cash (spent on buys, received from sells). PnL values whatever we hold at the current mid price:

Why "marked at mid"? If we hold 0.001 BTC, it's worth whatever BTC is worth now, so PnL moves every tick even with no trading. This is standard mark-to-market accounting, and it stops a losing position from being hidden.

Where profit comes from, and where it goes

Earned: the spread

Every time we buy on our bid and later sell on our ask, we earn the gap: here about — per BTC, so about — on our order size.

Lost: adverse selection

We tend to get filled right before the price moves against us, because traders with better information pick off stale quotes. It's measured by markout: the mid's move since each fill, in our favour. Recent average: —.

For market making to be profitable, spread earned plus rebates must exceed adverse selection plus fees. At a typical retail crypto fee of 0.1% per trade, a round trip on BTC costs about $170 per coin, far more than the cents of spread available. That's why this dashboard assumes an institutional fee tier (maker 0%, taker 0.02%). Real crypto market makers get zero or negative maker fees (rebates) through volume tiers and special programmes.

Why the PnL here is tiny and usually slightly negative: we trade only 0.001 BTC (about —) per order, over the public internet, tens of milliseconds from the exchange, with a textbook strategy. A small negative number is the realistic result. The value of this platform is that it measures exactly why the strategy loses (the markouts), which is the first thing a real desk does.

Technology

The tech stack, and why each piece

The rule: anything on the path from market data to an order is Rust. Everything else uses whichever tool fits the job best.

PieceWhat we useWhy this and not something else
Trading engine (book, strategy, risk, orders)RustAs fast as C++, with no garbage-collection pauses (unlike Java, Go, Python), and the compiler prevents memory corruption and data races. In trading, a memory bug loses money rather than just crashing.
Thread communicationOur own lock-free ring buffersLocks make threads wait on each other. A ring hands a message across CPU cores in about 8 ns, with no waiting and no memory allocation. Same idea as the LMAX Disruptor and Aeron used in finance.
Venue connectionstokio + tungstenite (WebSocket) + rustls (TLS)Networking is slow (milliseconds), so it runs on its own thread. The async runtime handles reconnects and timeouts without touching the engine thread.
Message parsingserde_json (zero-copy) + our exact decimal parserReads fields directly from the received bytes without copying, and converts prices to integers exactly.
Order matching simulatorRustA real price-time-priority exchange, for testing offline at about 86 ns per operation.
Recording (journal)Fixed 48-byte binary recordsRecords every event so any session can be replayed exactly: 14 million events per second, much faster than CSV or JSON.
Latency measurementHDR histogramsCaptures the full distribution (p50, p99, p99.9), not just averages, which hide the slow moments.
Web server and live feed to this pageaxum + WebSocket, 10 updates/sRuns on low-priority threads. The engine only drops a snapshot into a ring every 100 ms and never waits for the browser.
Console and this guidePlain HTML, CSS and JavaScriptNo build step; served straight from the engine binary. Would move to TypeScript + React as it grows.
Research and analysisPython + PolarsResearchers everywhere use Python. Polars has a Rust engine underneath, so analysis of millions of rows stays fast.

Where Rust isn't used, and why: notebooks and research (Python's ecosystem is unbeatable), user interfaces (the web is the right tool), and later FPGA hardware logic (that needs hardware languages such as SystemVerilog).

Architecture

How the pieces fit together

The program runs several threads at once, each with one job. Only the engine thread is allowed to change trading state. The others hand it messages through ring buffers, so nothing ever has to lock or wait.

Two other ways to run the same engine:

  • Simulator (adapter = "simulator"): a built-in exchange replaces the venue, with its own matching engine and simulated traders, some of whom know the "true" price. It works offline, and market making there is realistically hard.
  • tickrail replay: feeds a recorded journal or CSV file back through the strategy with new settings. It runs deterministically at millions of events per second, which is how you test changes before trading them.

What's already in place for scaling

  • One interface for every venue, so new exchanges plug in
  • Ring buffers between threads, which extend to processes and machines with Aeron
  • Linux CPU pinning, already written
  • Deterministic replay for testing every change
  • Binary journal as the audit trail

What must be built before real money

  • Authenticated order gateway (testnet first)
  • Position reconciliation with the exchange
  • Crash recovery from the journal
  • Alerts on gaps, rejects and losses
  • A strategy with positive markouts in replay over many days
  • Legal review of each exchange's terms and your jurisdiction
